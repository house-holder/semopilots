Piston Engines

Intro to Engines

A majority of piston engines (also called reciprocating engines) in aviation are gasoline-fueled, simple four- and six- cylinder designs that envolved with technology up to a point and simply stuck there. The truth is that for your average Lycoming or Continental engine on a Cessa, Piper, or even Cirrus? They aren't much more advanced than predecessors of the 1940s and 50s.

In the animation below, a very simplified four-cylinder engine is represented in very few components: a green crankshaft and flywheel, blue connecting rods, grey pistons, and half the crankcase in wire mesh structure. This is a relatively typical engine arrangement in trainer aircraft: two banks of cylinders are horizontally opposite of each other, or "opposed." Note that as the crankshaft moves through one 360-degree rotation, a given piston will move fully outward (or "upward" in the cylinder) and then fully inward/downward. Each movement back and forth is one reciprocation, or one stroke.

The engines making up the majority of what you find in the world today are four-stroke engines, meaning the crankshaft must move through two revolutions (720 degrees) to complete a full combustion cycle. See the following animation for a closer look at the complete cycle:

A breakdown of each stroke:

  1. Intake: The piston moves downward with the intake valve open, pulling fuel/air mixture (yellow) into the cylinder. This "suction" costs a small amount of power as the engine works to pump intake air.
  2. Compression: The piston moves upward and the intake valve closes to compress the fuel and air mixture. Compression raises temperature and costs a small amount of engine power.
  3. Power: The spark plug fires (red) and the resultant smooth, slow burn creates a rapid expansion that drives the piston downward. No explosions here!
  4. Exhaust: The combustion process runs out of available fuel or oxygen, and the leftovers of the reaction are noxious gases and heat (grey). The piston rises with the exhaust valve open for the final stroke, pushing exhaust out and preparing to repeat the cycle.

Other notes: the valve arrangement here is more typical of a modern car engine (or Diamond aircraft) than an aviation engine. The blue represents liquid cooling instead of far more conventional air cooling. At the bottom of the crankcase (below the crankshaft) is a yellow pool of engine oil in the oil sump, but the pump/lubricating oil throughout the engine is not depicted. The carburetor is depicted simply as well, showing only a vented float bowl full of pink fuel and a highly simplified venturi, main fuel jet, and throttle plate.

Any internal combusion engine (piston, turbine, rotary) is simply an air pump. The amount of air pumped determines, among other things, power output and efficiency. The reason we call it a throttle (and the reason "gas pedal" is a misnomer in your car) is because it throttles or restricts the engine's air supply. But ignoring the variable air density available, we can measure the amount of air an engine can theoretically displace as the total volume of all cylinders.

This volume in the US is most often expressed as cubic inches, but in the automotive world may also be expressed as liters. The common engine of a 1970s Cessna is the Lycoming O-320, an Opposed, 320-cubic-inch-displacement engine. This total displacement number divided by the number of cylinders is the per-cylinder displacement, which means each cylinder's swept volume is roughly 80 cubic inches.

The cutaway above is of a slightly smaller Lycoming O-290, but the basic layout is identical to others. At the near side, we can see the propeller flange where the flywheel and prop mount up. You can take a look into a cylinder, and even see an intake valve in this cylinder. Above the prop flange, you can see the crankshaft and above it the camshaft that operates the valves.

What should I really know?

Engines are perhaps one of the most dense systems in all of aviation. This topic could easily span hours of discussion (especially if you're being interrogated by a mechanic), but you should know basic facts about your engine:

  • What specific steps of your preflight confirm the engine is ready to fly?
  • How does a carburetor work? What controls does it have? What drawbacks? (Or if not carbureted: How about fuel injection?)
  • What sort of ignition system does your engine have? How is it connected and controlled? How do you check this system before flight?
  • What type of oil system does your engine have? What function does oil serve in your engine?
  • What other limitations or operating/handling instructions exist for your engine?

Some of the questions above can be expanded into even deeper knowledge for Commercial or CFI-level study. The rest of this page will dive into that level of detail. So if you came seeking deeper knowledge, this is the right place.

Deeper Engine Study

Three parts of combustion

There are three basic ingredients to ANY combustion event (yes, including your charcoal BBQ cookout): oxygen, fuel, and a source of heat or ignition. This is what chemists (or firefighters, or mechanics...) refer to as the "fire triangle" - take any one of these away, and combustion doesn't happen. This idea may start to connect to some of your emergency memory items, like an engine/induction system fire during start.

The remainder of this discussion will aim at breaking down engines in terms of either these three primary concerns, or "extras." Extras will include items like oil systems, control systems and schemes, and general knowledge about engine technology that may be interesting tangents to take as independent study.

Getting Oxygen: Induction Systems

The "oxygen" side of this equation is relatively straightforward for us: either our engines are naturally aspirated, meaning they have to breathe whatever air density is available to them at the temperature and altitude we're using; or they're forced induction engines: turbocharged and supercharged engines that have a way to force-feed a bit of extra air into the induction system. The simplest form of forced induction is a blower or mechanical supercharger, a simple spinning air pump driven by the engine:

This device can also be referred to as a simple mechanical compressor. Often paired to the engine at approximately a 10:1 gear ratio - so it turns 20,000RPM if the engine is turning 2,000 RPM. The basic supercharger/compressor of this type costs a bit of engine power to run, but force-feeds the engine a sufficiently dense amount of extra air to outweigh this cost.

There are a couple of downsides to superchargers. Compressing air adds heat to the induction system, which both decreases power efficiency and brings the engine closer to the range where detonation (explosions or uncontrolled combustion) is more likely. The fixed mechanical supercharger also just adds a boost of power that is relatively constant across all altitudes. This means that much like a naturally aspirated engine, we will slowly lose power as we continue to climb higher into lower-density air.

The turbocharger can be a bit more useful to us for a few reasons, but not without some additional costs and considerations. Let's first discuss the term: aviation tends to call these systems "turbo superchargers" - so why? What does that mean?

To expand the term more completely, we could call them turbine powered superchargers. Think of the average windmill, or wind turbine you see standing amongst the cattle and generating power: what is it actually doing? In terms of mechanical principles, it is taking the flow of a gas (wind) and converting this into mechanical power (rotating a shaft). We use that shaft to turn a generator in the wind turbine, but in a turbocharger we use it for something else.

As the above simple drawing shows: exhaust gas blows over the hot turbine side of the system, the shaft from the turbine turns a compressor wheel, and this compressor forces air into the induction system of our engine. Sounds nearly identical to a plain supercharger, but with one additional level of control: we can now decide how much exhaust flow is routed to the turbine through a control system that positions a wastegate valve.

In the diagram above, you can see that a similar depiction to the throttle valve is placed near the wastegate label. This simplified drawing drives at the point of a wastegate: it is an easier gas path for exhaust to follow than to move through the turbine wheel. With a wastegate fully open, the turbine wheel does not extract as much power from the exhaust, and the compressor does not provide significant compression to the intake air. The engine "draws" air through the compressor wheel freely, and is essentially acting much like a naturally aspirated version of itself.

But as the wastegate closes, more and more engine exhaust is forced to flow over the turbine wheel. If the engine is running at sufficient speed, this exhaust gas generates enough power that the compressor is capable of boosting the pressure in the engine intake manifold above ambient; instead of roughly 30.0"Hg it may see 35 or even 40" at of manifold pressure. This would be turbo-supercharging, or turbocharging for short, the induction system.

Some alternative turbocharging control arrangements instead just attempt to maintain some lower setpoint - say, sea level pressure of roughly 30"Hg. This would be referred to as turbo-normalizing. At first that seems silly - isn't every engine capable of reaching this pressure at sea level? Yep! So the turbocharger isn't working as hard at lower altitudes. But as your altitude increases (or really as density overall decreases), the wastegate will be controlled a bit more closed in order to maintain sea level pressure, which it can now do to a much higher altitude.

Regardless of truly turbocharged or turbo-normalized, an engine using these systems is now referred to as an altitude engine (remember §91.205?). This just means the engine now has a defined critical altitude - that is, an altitude to which the engine can maintain its TCDS horsepower rating. In common language, this is the kind of engine that could provide sufficient power for a much higher aircraft service ceiling. In addition, the engine will likely consume significantly more fuel.

There are a few unique considerations for turbocharged engines. Firstly: the exhaust gas temperatures you see will be significantly higher, and will sometimes be referred to as Turbine Inlet Temperature instead of EGT. The turbine wheel getting in the way causes quite a bit of heat buildup, and we want to avoid melting it down. This temperature also has a direct impact on the conditions faced by the exhaust valves.

Second: While there may be robust automatic control systems in place, you may have to carefully set takeoff manifold pressure (MP) yourself! Some aircraft have the ability to over-boost slightly above takeoff limits. With training and careful reading of your flight manuals, this should be no problem. But as you can see above: A redline of 46" and an upper green arc value of roughly 39.5" likely represent two operational limitations - I'd assume max MP and perhaps max continuous or climb MP value, respectively.

The sneaky factor that yet again appears here, just like in supercharging, is the pesky law of physics that states compressing a working gas leads to increased heat. As mentioned earlier, this increased heat is a detonation risk as well as a general contributor to inefficiency. So despite the overall gains of having forced induction at all, we want to try and cool down the charge air in our induction system if we can.

In the image above, you can follow the path of intake air: the filter is right behind the prop, at the front of the engine baffles. From there a pipe descends to the turbo's compressor inlet, and from the compressor housing a white pipe ascends to the small white box at the rear of the engine baffling. Air then moves through the horizontal white pipe to the intake system atop the engine.

The white box is a charge air cooler, or intercooler. It's an air-to-air heat exchanger - so the somewhat-warm air flooding the upper cowl is forced through this intercooler and down into the lower cowl, taking away some of the heat of compression that was added by the compressor down below. This increases the engine's detonation margins and provides a slight gain in engine efficiency for little overall mechanical cost - weight and expense not counted.
