Advanced Engine Technology

What's in a modern engine?

Among other things, aviation has been slow to adopt modern electronic technology in engines. Many of the systems in your car have low impact when they fail. Perhaps a check engine light comes on, and with any serious failures you end up on the side of the road. But in aviation, we don't exactly have convenient place to pull over. Setting up excess dependencies across many separate systems adds risk in a way that many have pushed back against for a long time. But what can we do to mitigate the risk and still reap the rewards of efficiency and simplicity?

A modern gasoline-fueled car may offer several systems that the traditional aviation engine lacks:

  • A common-rail, high pressure fuel injection system that is conditionally capable of highly efficient lean-of-peak (LOP) fuel scheduling
  • Control over ignition timing to ensure combustion propagation is appropriate to the engine load (available air) and piston speeds
  • Control over turbocharger/wastegate operation to avoid exceeding engine or turbo limits
  • Variable cam timing or phasing to extract power more fully at higher engine RPM

There are of course drawbacks in terms of certification cost, weight, complexity, etc. But the biggest drawback that automotive systems don't solve is the problem of redundancy: Despite the low failure rate, most vehicles have only one engine control unit, or ECU. The ignition system is often a capacitive discharge (CD) design, drawing electrical power directly from the battery. There are often single-point critical sensors, where one sensor failing results in the full loss of engine control and power. So what can be done to mitigate these concerns?

The first solution comes from switching fuels: instead of gasoline, a common advanced engine in aviation uses the Diesel cycle. Unlike a gas engine, a diesel engine does not require any dedicated ignition system. Simply injecting high-pressure fuel directly into the cylinder spontaneously ignites diesel (or in our case, Jet A) due to the heat of compression and the latent heat left from the previous combustion event. The drawback is that the engine is much harder to start when cold, but adding a glowplug system to automatically heat things up for starting is a minor detail. Better yet, this system does not critically affect in-flight operation.

An inline four-cylinder (I4) animation, much like the Austro series used in Diamond NG-series aircraft.

Regarding other solutions: make everything in pairs! Instead of one ECU, the Austro series of aviation engines uses two of them, and they are allowed to crosstalk in a way that monitors the health of each. They are used in turn, checked independently (and automatically) prior to flight, and they are fed by redundant sensors that report signal degradation long before full failure.

There are still a few single points of failure, but even conventional engines bear these in abundance. Added complexity, however, is where these modern engines begin to look a bit concerning:

On the front of the slanted, I4 engine design is a prop flange coming out of a reduction gearbox. The simple fact is that the relatively small displacement of these engines (121 cubic inches for the AE300 series) means they cannot produce the same power as the 300-inch (or greater) behemoths of yesteryear without increasing engine RPM. So for the propeller output of 2,300RPM, the engine must end up turning nearly 3,900RPM to make its rated 168 horsepower.

Reduction gearboxes add a few negatives: additional weight, another oil system to monitor or service, and a part that takes the full force of the engine and must be inspected or replaced based on operating criteria. The gearbox oil is able to be used in prop governing, and has a predictably cooler service life leading to longer replacement intervals.

A generic common rail fuel injection system. Note the return fuel heading back to the fuel tank, and schematic representation of control systems linked to the control unit (ECU)

Another single point of failure would be found in the high pressure fuel pump used for the common rail injection system. The fuel injectors depend on incredibly high pressures - max 23,000psi - to do their job, and the high pressure pump (regulated by the ECU) provides this rail pressure with no backup. To alleviate some of the pump's load and reap a few small rewards, the common rail pressure is regulated in a continuous-return fashion. Excess fuel beyond the needs of the engine is returned to the fuel tanks, and is slightly warmer after being pressurized. This fuel passes through the fuel tanks and provides a modest amount of tank heating, offsetting the cooling effect of altitude that would otherwise slightly decrease efficiency.

The last major failure mode introduced in these modern engines is something completely absent from conventional engines: liquid cooling. Using a thermostatic system much like the average on-road vehicle, coolant is kept under pressure (which raises the boiling point) and circulated with an engine-driven coolant pump. As the coolant temperature approaches a thermostatic setpoint, the thermostat valve opens and begins circulating the coolant to the radiator where cowl air will pull heat overboard.

There are some distinct advantages to liquid cooling, but first the drawbacks: a loss of coolant represents a loss of the engine, but with a small period of awareness as levels drop and engine temp increases. The other drawback aside from complexity is the weight: the system detracts from useful load significantly. It also increases maintenance requirements and serves as another system to preflight.

But what about the advantages? Liquid cooled engines run at more consistent internal temperatures (read: metal temps) which leads to more controlled thermal expansion than in air-cooled counterparts. This means the engines can be manufactured to closer tolerances, leading to things like less overall oil consumption/contamination as well as better combustion efficiency across the full operating range.

In addition, just like in your car: the coolant can be used in a separate heater core through which cabin air can be heated for climate control. This is safer than the exhaust manifold exchangers present in traditional engines, and carries virtually zero risk compared to the potential carbon monoxide concerns of older engines. Also: this heat will be available sooner, even at idle power, whereas the older systems simply don't warm until the exhaust begins absorbing the heat output of an engine at full power on departure.
