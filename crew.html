Crewed Flight Ops

Logging PIC Crew Flights

The Commercial Single-Engine curriculum (rev 10 at time of writing) provides students the opportunity to fly with one another during time building, including some procedures for sharing the flight workload. Students may simultaneously (carefully) log flight time.

This time is logged under the requirements of (among others) §91.109(c), and requires specific hours be included or omitted depending on which student is in which role.

The Gebhart LOI (letter of interpretation) clarifies that cross country time can only be logged by the pilot flying. This means you have to be a little careful about how you track and log the time to remain legal.

Splitting Hours

Let's run through a logging scenario where two students named Joe and Amy are flying a PIC Crewed cross-country flight from Cape to St. Louis Downtown (CPS). Joe will be pilot flying (PF) for leg 1.

There are a few extra things they need to record to ensure they can accurately track hours:

  • Joe's foggle time for leg 1
  • Hobbs hours when they switch
  • Amy's foggle time for leg 2

So the two finish the flight, they've collected those data points above, and now it's time to figure out their logbook situations. Here's the outcome, and how the time can be logged:

  • Hobbs start: 200.0
  • Leg 1: PF Joe logs 48min/0.8 under foggles.
  • Shutdown in CPS: Hobbs reads 201.2 and they switch.
  • Leg 2: PF Amy logs 1.0 under foggles.
  • Hobbs stop: 202.7 (2.7hrs on the airplane)

For the first leg, Joe logs all 1.2 as cross country, PIC, and TT. He also logs the takeoff and landing in addition to 0.8 hood. As safety pilot, Amy is only allowed to log 0.8 PIC and TT, but logs no XC time or landings.


For the second leg, Amy logs the remaining 1.5 as XC, PIC, and TT. She logs TO/LDG and the 1.0 hood. As safety pilot, Joe logs 1.0 PIC and TT, but must log zero XC time and no landings.


In total, Joe logs 1.2 + 1.0 = 2.2hrs of flying. Amy logs 0.8 + 1.5 = 2.3hrs of flying.

Neither pilot will capture the full 2.7 of airplane time, but the claimed advantage is cheaper hours for the time splitters and this scheme definitely helps out with our aircraft availability.


It should be clear from the description above that the "easy" thing to do to keep the logbooks clean is to divide each flight into two separate log entries. Don't forget to also record the name of your safety pilot alongside your PF entry as required by §61.51(b).
