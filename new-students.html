Welcome to Cape!

The following will be a bit of reading, if you can stand it:

Learning to fly will become pretty easy once you've spent a number of hours doing it. Learning to learn, however, can be a struggle at first. We have a lot of knowledge and resources at our disposal, and the expectation is that you will seek them out and self-start your learning in many ways to go above and beyond the minimum needed to pass classes, exams, and check events during training.

What is a flying career?

At the simplest level, a job flying an airplane sounds great: sit in the front seats, look outside, visit new places. It's often touted as a luxurious lifestyle, and for good reason. Aviation, while taking a lot of inspiration from the marine industry in terms of comfort and class, totally redefined how we view travel. It was faster, more sleek, more advanced than anything else we had. But it was not without risk.

You're introduced to the concept of risk very early in training. One of your first big encounters with it happens when you first solo. Of course your instructor has assessed the risk and decided that you must have the capability and qualification to fly on your own - but now it's just you making the decisions.

The good news is that we methodically step through every decision - the way we feel regarding our personal physiology, the weather for the day, the aircraft's airworthiness, and even the second-to-second operations of an aircraft - with a structure and long-standing processes in place. At first, these things can seem a little overwhelming.

But it's only overwhelming because you're getting used to the complete picture of this assessment, and after enough experience applying the skill the full picture becomes much more clear. Weather will vary from place to place as you move around the world, but you will know yourself better regarding your readiness. The environment, while also changing, will largely follow similar patterns: how airports are built, how ATC tends to talk to you, and how you expect other aircraft to operate in your vicinity. The last question mark becomes your airplane.

Your Airplane

"Your airplane", "your controls", "you have the controls." This phrase takes on meaning of "it's time for me to fly" early on in training, but I would encourage you to think about it a little harder than that.

"Your airplane" should give you the feeling of possessing a bit more than just the flight controls. At the very least, you should now feel in possession of at least some of the decisionmaking - where is the airplane going? How is it negotiating the mild bumps of a warm summer day? When is it time to begin a descent towards the destination?

We of course have checklists, policies, and guidelines on how to make these decisions. But they're still yours to make, and the airplane is still your airplane to learn. This seems like the crucial point: your responsibility for the aircraft is to learn it, and well.

The Pilot's Operating Handbook for the majority of the fleet we train in (a 1976 edition for the Cessna 172M) has 117 scanned pages in PDF form on the school's Document Library. Some number of these pages are not information you read and absorb, but instead charts you use, or an equipment list that has since been revised with panel updates. That being said, almost every digital page is a scan of two pages from the original book.

By approximation, you may estimate no more than two hundred pages are "reading" material to learn the aircraft. The rest you will learn by working with the airplane ("your controls!"), gaining hours and experience by flying and adapting to new tasks. You will learn by (hopefully) asking well-informed questions. And in moments where you do not have someone to answer them for you, you will learn by finding an answer entirely on your own.

Your Future Airplane

Some students dream of flying heavy metal - the Boeing 777, the Airbus A330, an MD11. Maybe you're more into regional airlines, and want to see about squeezing yourself into the 737 at Southwest. Perhaps you can see yourself in a fast-mover - the F-16, F-22. Or maybe you want to try out the corporate world and fly aircraft with no standing room: Gulfstreams, Citations, Falcons. Maybe none of the above!

Regardless of your interest, it is a guarantee there will be reading. Speaking from experience: the A320-series Flight Manual at one airline is 2,671 pages. The 777 version is 2,175 pages - but there are also manuals on how long-range international ops work. So the job may be Flying*, but that asterisk can carry quite a bit of reading.

But you've made it to this point, so you are capable of doing the reading. Your flying aptitude, your ability to remain motivated, and your ability to self-start when necessary are all essential to this process. It all begins with your persistence in absorbing and understanding information, and the learning truly never ends.

Your Path Forward

There may be some struggles, some hang-ups along the way. And while we all have unique experiences, you can bet someone - whether CFI, another flight student, or just another person outside of training - has gone through at least one facet of the difficulty you encounter.

So reach out to people. Be your own best resource, but do not hesitate to seek others when things get difficult. This industry rests on the robust support systems built over the last hundred-plus years; Systems built by people that recognized we must all pull together on a common rope to achieve the safe, lucrative, and comfortable careers aviation can offer. And if you see someone else struggling, offer that support you'd want available for yourself.

And at the end of the day, enjoy the ride. Like many things, aviation gives out something equal to what you put into it. If you're meant for this career, it will reward you. Maybe not exactly in the way you'd planned or hoped - but as you'll find out, deviating from a plan is not only normal, it's often expected and sometimes necessary.

Fly safe!
